Career in the Morning, Game After That

August 21, 2026

career startups games

I am having trouble right now.

Not catastrophic trouble. Not the kind where everything is visibly falling apart and the correct response is obvious. That would almost be easier, in its own unpleasant way. This is quieter and more irritating. I have two possible futures in front of me, both of them difficult, both of them uncertain, and both of them asking for more attention than I can sustainably give.

On one side is my career. I wanted to keep building it after leaving Refilled. That was the sensible path. I had done the startup CTO thing. I had spent years building systems, teams, products, hardware, software, process, and whatever other flaming pile of responsibility was thrown onto the table. The obvious next move was to take that experience into a larger organisation and continue climbing.

The problem is that the market is not especially interested in obvious next moves.

At my level, finding the right role was never going to be quick. Senior hiring is slow at the best of times, and this is not the best of times. The market feels clogged. Companies are nervous. Hiring processes are slow, defensive, and often poorly designed. Agentic AI has made the application flood worse, which means the whole machine is now processing an even larger pile of low-quality signal while the actual humans involved slowly lose the will to live. Naturally, everyone responds by adding more process, because apparently the solution to mud is bureaucracy.

After a few months, I stopped pretending I was on a short break and picked up an old project.

Deepfield.

It was not a small distraction. It was one of the big ones. The kind of project that has history, emotional weight, technical depth, and enough unfinished ambition to make it dangerous. I told myself, reasonably enough, that I needed something to get me up in the morning. Something to work on while the job market took its time deciding whether I existed.

For three months I have been unemployed and working on the game.

That has been good for me in some ways. It gave my days structure. It gave me a mission. It stopped me from collapsing into the sofa and letting my brain hold another little tribunal about my future. I know myself well enough to know that idle time becomes dangerous when life is unstable. I need work. I need a problem. I need something large enough to pull my attention forward.

The game did that.

But it also created a new problem. From a marketing perspective, I am starting from zero. I have built the playtest, but getting people to actually play it has been much harder than expected. The only people who have started it up so far are people I have strong relationships with, and even then the energy has mostly been “I’ll do this for you” rather than “I want to play this.” That distinction matters. Gratitude is nice, but it is not demand. Friendship can get someone to install a game once. It cannot build a market.

So now I have two hard problems instead of one.

The career path is not working yet. After three full interview cycles, I still do not have a role. One process I outright pissed them off with my performance. The other two didn’t feel like fair rejections, or worse, they felt like legally safe versions of the real reason. Whether that instinct is correct or not, the pattern has been hard to ignore. I have startup experience, seniority, and a fairly unusual way of thinking, and instead of feeling valuable, those things currently make me feel strangely unemployable.

That is an ugly sentence to write.

The game path is not working yet either. I have the project, the experience, the taste, the technical ability, and years of accumulated awareness about games. It is the least cold start available to me. The rational part of my brain looks at that and says: build your own thing. You are already wired like an owner. You have spent years collecting the skills for this. Stop trying to contort yourself into roles where people seem uneasy because you do not quack like the obedient Engineering Manager they imagined after reading a Scrum certification brochure.

There is truth in that.

There is also memory.

The emotional part of me hears “build your own thing” and immediately replies: you did this before. You were not good at the part that mattered. You spent years trying to make games, failed to make them work commercially, and came out the other side depressed, aimless, and increasingly difficult to distinguish from furniture. It was your wife and Refilled that pulled you out of that abyss. Do you really want to walk back toward it because the job market made you feel unwelcome for a few months?

That is also true.

This is the unpleasant part of major life changes. The mind does not merely consider the future. It drags every past wound into the room and asks whether we would like a live reenactment. The rational argument and the emotional memory are both trying to protect me, but they are protecting me from different dangers. One sees the opportunity cost of waiting for employment in a bad market. The other sees the old shape of obsession, isolation, and failure.

The result is existential dread.

I know, from experience, that these moments pass. I know there will be some point in the future where I look back and wonder why I was even worried. Not because the problem was imaginary, but because the state I am in now will not be the state I am in forever. The heaviness will move. The facts will change. Something will open. Some path I can’t currently see will become obvious in hindsight, and I will have the unbearable privilege of acting as if it was always going to happen.

But from inside the storm, you don’t see the blue sky. You feel wet, miserable, and a little silly.

I know that if I do the work and weather this, things will be better on the other side. Not necessarily in the way I can imagine now. Probably not in the way I would even choose. But better in the old, annoying, retrospective sense. Better because the struggle will force some change I would not have made voluntarily. Better because discomfort has a habit of doing what comfort politely avoids.

That knowledge helps, but it does not solve the scheduling problem.

My stated priority is employment. My actual time allocation says otherwise. I can tell myself that finding a role matters most, but then I spend sixteen hours a day working on the game, marketing the game, thinking about the game, improving the game, watching videos about how to market games, or wandering around the edge of the game like a raccoon inspecting a locked bin. The calendar is more honest than the self-image. Right now, my calendar says the game is the priority.

That is a problem.

The tempting answer is to embrace it. Stop pretending. Switch priorities. Build the audience. Push the game properly. Take seriously what people keep telling me: you should start your own thing.

There is a reason that advice lands. I do walk, talk, and think like an owner. I do not seem naturally shaped for sitting quietly inside someone else’s operating model, asking permission to use judgement. I suspect that is part of what has made some interviews feel strange. The people on the other side may be looking for someone to manage within the existing process, keep the rituals running, and not disturb the furniture too much. Then I arrive with founder scar tissue, systems opinions, and a habit of asking whether the process is helping the work or just keeping everyone emotionally sedated.

Unconfirmed, obviously.

But I have learned to trust my gut when a room starts to feel uneasy.

The typical advice here would be discipline. Be sensible. Allocate time properly. Do not chase the emotionally rewarding project while the financially important project sits neglected. That advice is probably correct, which is rude of it.

The game is a probability play. The industry is brutally competitive. Even if I execute well, I may still fail to generate attention, revenue, or anything resembling a sustainable business. Good games fail all the time. Interesting games fail all the time. Games with better marketing, better art, better teams, better timing, and better luck fail all the time. The market does not owe me discovery because I built something difficult.

It would be foolish to burn the rest of my savings on another attempt at the games industry just because employment feels humiliating right now. That would not be bravery. That would be fear wearing a founder hoodie.

So the answer, for now, has to be boring and concrete.

Mornings are for the career.

Two or three hours every day go to outreach, applications, messages, coffees, recruiters, former colleagues, warm contacts, cold contacts, and whatever other little humiliations are required to remind the market I exist. Email. SMS. LinkedIn. Coffee. Follow-ups. The cup goes out. The cup rattles. Very dignified, naturally, but dignity has limited utility when the pipeline is empty.

After that, the game can have the rest of the day.

That structure does not solve the existential problem, but it does solve the immediate one. It stops the game from eating the entire day while still giving it enough room to matter. It stops the job search from becoming a vague background guilt cloud and turns it into a daily act. It creates a small fence between two competing futures so they don’t spend all day trampling each other.

This is not the inspiring version of ambition. It is not “quit everything and follow your dream.” It is also not “be responsible and abandon the foolish creative thing.” It is the less cinematic middle: keep both alive, but stop lying about priority.

The hard part is that both paths feel low probability right now.

The hiring market feels cooked. The games market is a thunderdome with Steam tags. Employment requires convincing someone that my particular combination of experience, seniority, and owner-brain is an asset rather than a threat. Deepfield requires building not only the game, but an audience from almost nothing. Neither path is easy. Neither path offers certainty. Both require personal growth, and frankly I would prefer if personal growth came in smaller, less expensive packets.

I even considered applying to be a bus driver.

Not as a joke, exactly. More as a reminder that the mind, when stressed, begins shopping for exits. Any exit. Something concrete. Something simple. Something with a roster and a vehicle and a defined route. Of course, they would probably look at my driving history and tell me to beat it, which would at least be funny in the grim little way life occasionally remembers pacing.

The truth is that I do not know which path wins.

I do not know whether the next role appears in three weeks or three months. I do not know whether the game finds an audience or continues to sit there like an elaborate machine nobody asked to see. I do not know whether the thing that makes me valuable is currently making me hard to hire. I do not know whether this period is the beginning of a new independent chapter or just the awkward valley between jobs.

What I do know is that uncertainty punishes passivity.

If I do nothing, the fear gets louder. If I let the game consume everything, the career path decays. If I spend all day job hunting, I become miserable and useless by lunchtime. If I try to solve the entire future at once, I become paralysed by imaginary timelines.

So I am reducing the problem to the day.

In the morning, I work the employment path. I make contact with the market. I put myself in motion. I do the uncomfortable social work that does not feel natural but is required because opportunities rarely emerge from silent competence alone.

Then I build.

I work on the game. I improve the product. I learn the marketing. I make the videos. I try to turn the thing from a private obsession into something with a public surface. I do that knowing it may fail, because the alternative is letting the project rot and resenting myself for being too scared to push it.

This is not clarity. Not yet.

It is a way to stay alive inside ambiguity.

Maybe that is all this stage requires. Not a grand decision about the next ten years. Not a clean identity as founder, employee, game developer, executive, failure, genius, fraud, or any of the other costumes the brain tries on at 1 a.m. Just enough structure to keep both doors open while the facts change.

I have been through enough large life changes to know that the storm passes. I have also been through enough to know that knowing this does not make the rain feel dry. You still have to stand in it. You still have to make the next day workable. You still have to resist the urge to turn fear into prophecy.

So that is the plan.

Career in the morning. Game after that.

Not because it guarantees the future, but because it keeps me moving long enough for the future to reveal itself.
