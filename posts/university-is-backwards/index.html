Universities Did Not Make Smart People

July 3, 2026

careers economics

We have developed a strange idea about universities.

The modern belief is that a university takes a person, puts them through a four-year process, charges them enough money to keep several layers of administrators breathing comfortably, and then releases them into the world transformed. Before university, they were ordinary. After university, they are educated, capable, employable, and ready to solve hard problems because a respected institution has printed their name in a serious font.

That is the story we tell now, but it is not quite the story that made universities valuable in the first place.

The older version was much simpler. Prestigious universities were desirable because they selected talented people. The degree had weight because admission had weight. If someone got into Harvard, Oxford, Cambridge, MIT, or any of the other names people like to deploy slowly at dinner parties, the signal was not merely that they had sat in lecture halls and submitted assignments. The signal was that they had been chosen from a large group of people who wanted to be there and were not chosen.

That distinction matters because it changes where the value comes from.

A prestigious university did not need to manufacture brilliance from nothing. It needed to identify enough brilliant, ambitious, unusually capable people early, place them near each other, expose them to serious standards, and attach its name to whatever they went on to do. Over time the institution gained prestige from the output of the people it selected, and future selection became easier because ambitious people wanted access to that prestige. The university and the students reinforced each other.

Somewhere along the way, that mechanism became confused with transformation. We started talking as if the institution itself was the source of the ability. Attend the right place, complete the course, receive the credential, and the market should treat you as capable. The qualification became proof not only that you had been taught something, but that you had become something.

The strongest defenders of university usually retreat to the serious professions: medicine, engineering, law, science, mathematics. These are the fields where formal education is supposed to be beyond question. But even there, selection still does more work than people want to admit.

The person who becomes a good doctor was usually not made intelligent by medical school. They were selected because they were already unusually capable, disciplined, conscientious, and able to survive years of brutal filtering. The person who becomes a good engineer was not granted mathematical reasoning by the university like a wizard handing out a quest item. They had the underlying ability, temperament, and tolerance for hard abstraction before the institution attached itself to the process.

Formal education can transmit bodies of knowledge. It can expose people to standards, methods, techniques, and professional norms. It can reduce dangerous ignorance in fields where mistakes can kill people, collapse bridges, or bankrupt clients. But that is not the same as manufacturing the person capable of using that knowledge well.

The institution trains the selected. It does not create them from nothing.

A university can train, expose, refine, discipline, credential, and occasionally inspire. It can provide the environment in which a capable person becomes far more capable. What it cannot reliably do is manufacture intelligence, curiosity, judgement, taste, intellectual honesty, or the instinct to wrestle with a hard problem until it gives up its secrets. Those qualities tend to exist before the institution gets involved. The university may amplify them, but it does not create them on demand.

This is why two people can pass through the same institution and emerge with wildly different value. One student treats the university as an amplifier. They use the lectures, peers, professors, books, arguments, labs, and deadlines as a way to sharpen themselves. Another treats it as a transaction. Attend enough classes, submit acceptable work, satisfy the bureaucratic machinery, collect the credential, move on. Same campus, same course catalogue, same logo on the certificate, completely different outcome.

The expansion of higher education made this confusion worse.

As degrees became more common, they became less informative. Employers began requiring them for roles that did not obviously need them. Parents pushed children toward university because not going seemed reckless. Governments subsidised and moralised the whole structure. Universities expanded, diversified their offerings, hired more administrators, built more buildings, and discovered that the demand for legitimacy is a reliable business model.

The degree slowly shifted from evidence of selection into a ticket for entry. For many roles, it no longer signalled exceptional ability. It signalled that the person had completed the expected path. That is not worthless, but it is a weaker signal than people pretend. It may indicate discipline, literacy, persistence, and the ability to navigate institutions. It may also indicate that someone was willing to accumulate debt because every adult around them insisted this was the responsible thing to do.

The old signal was that a difficult filter had selected this person. The new signal is often that this person completed an expensive product.

Those are not the same thing.

The most prestigious institutions still preserve more of the old model because selection remains part of the signal. If a school rejects almost everyone, admission itself communicates something. The teaching may be excellent or uneven. The course may be transformative or merely competent. But the filtering function remains powerful because the institution can still say, implicitly, that most people did not get in.

For much of higher education, that filtering power has weakened while the price has increased. That is a very convenient arrangement for the institution and a much less convenient one for the student, who is asked to buy a credential whose market value is no longer as clear as the sales pitch implies.

This creates credential inflation. Jobs that once required competence begin requiring degrees. Degrees that once signalled unusual ability begin signalling baseline compliance. Students respond by pursuing more qualifications. Employers respond by raising the bar again. The whole thing becomes an arms race in which everyone has more paperwork, more debt, and not necessarily more ability.

There is a more personal version of this for me, and it is the part that makes me angry.

Australian universities now operate with a massive marketing machine aimed at foreign students. The promise is simple: come here, pay the money, get the degree, and this qualification will give you an advantage. For a long time, that was probably true. The name carried weight. The credential opened doors. The university could sell status because the market still believed the status meant something.

But a credential only has value if the people reading it still care.

As a hiring manager, I no longer care about university qualifications. I do not look at a degree and think, “This person must be capable.” I do not see the name of the institution and assume some meaningful filter has already done the work for me. That signal has been diluted so badly that in many cases it has inverted.

I will choose the person without the degree over the person with one.

Not as a cute contrarian hiring trick. Not because education is bad. Because the people who present the degree as proof of competence have, in my experience, so often failed the moment the conversation moved from institutional approval to reality. They could tell me where they studied. They could tell me what qualification they held. Then I would ask them to reason through an actual problem, explain a trade-off, describe something they had built, or show evidence of independent thought, and the whole structure would collapse.

That is where the signal breaks.

If someone has no degree, I am forced to look at the work. What have they built? How do they think? What have they solved? Where did they struggle? What did they learn without being spoon-fed a path? What evidence exists that this person can sit in front of a real problem, with no rubric, no lecturer, no marking guide, and still make progress?

That is the real test.

The tragedy is that the people most damaged by credential inflation are not the lazy ones. They are the capable ones. The intelligent, productive, self-directed people who can actually do the work but do not carry the right institutional badge. Those are the people the old system was supposed to identify. Those are the people elite institutions built their reputations on selecting. The powerhouses. The outliers. The people who make things happen.

Now they get left out to dry because the market is flooded with paper signals sold at scale.

That is the actual grift. It is not simply that universities became expensive. It is that they sold access to a signal they did not create. They inherited the prestige of selection, converted it into a product, diluted it through mass credentialing, and then left employers, students, and genuinely capable outsiders to deal with the wreckage.

The university marketing department can keep promising advantage. The brochures can keep showing clean buildings, smiling students, and the implied glow of future professional legitimacy. But if the person on the other side of the interview table has learned through repeated experience that the degree tells them almost nothing useful, then the credential has already failed.

Worse than failed.

It has become noise.

And when a signal becomes noise, the people who suffer are the ones who were actually worth finding.

Universities sell more than education. They sell hope, identity, legitimacy, and access. They sell the idea that if you step onto the conveyor belt and stay there long enough, you will emerge respectable. For some people, that promise is fulfilled. For others, it becomes a very expensive lesson in the difference between being credentialed and being useful.

Usefulness is still tested by reality, which remains stubbornly unimpressed by stationery.

A degree does not debug the system. It does not close the deal, design the bridge, lead the team, calm the customer, repair the machine, make the trade-off, or notice that the entire plan depends on an assumption nobody has tested. People do those things. Sometimes those people have degrees. Sometimes they do not. The credential may open the door, and in some professions it absolutely should, but it does not do the work once inside.

This is especially visible in software, startups, trades, operations, and other fields where output becomes visible quickly. The person either builds, understands, learns, ships, reasons, adapts, and collaborates, or they do not. A degree may tell you something about their path, but it cannot replace direct evidence of ability. Show me what they have built. Show me how they think. Show me how they respond when the problem stops matching the textbook. That is where the real signal begins.

The best version of university is still valuable, but only if we understand what it is actually doing. It gathers capable people, gives them structure, exposes them to serious ideas, surrounds them with peers, and places them under pressure from standards that existed before their feelings entered the room. For the right person, that can be transformative. But the transformation depends heavily on what the person brings into the system.

A university is not a factory that turns ordinary input into exceptional output. It is closer to a multiplier. If the student brings curiosity, discipline, and ability, the institution can amplify those qualities. If the student brings only a desire for certification, the institution can still provide certification, because apparently civilisation required a prestige-themed receipt printer.

This is the distinction we keep losing. Harvard did not become Harvard because its lecture slides contained ancient magic. It became Harvard because it attracted, selected, concentrated, and branded talented people across generations. The name gained power because the people attached to the name kept doing powerful things. The institution mattered, but so did the selection mechanism that fed it.

When universities forget this, they start believing the credential creates the value by itself. When students forget it, they start believing attendance is the same as development. When employers forget it, they use degrees as lazy proxies for judgement. When society forgets it, we push young people into debt for qualifications that may not make them smarter, more capable, or more useful.

The question is not whether universities matter. Some matter a great deal. The question is what part of them matters. If the value comes from selection, we should be honest about that. If the value comes from training, the training should be judged by outcomes. If the value comes from networking, we should stop pretending the whole thing is purely about education. If the value comes from signalling class, compliance, and institutional approval, then perhaps we should at least have the decency to admit the machine is less noble than the brochure.

Universities can still be valuable, but they do not make smart people by default. At their best, they find capable people, sharpen them, surround them with others, and give the world a useful signal. At their worst, they sell an expensive credential to people who were told from childhood that buying one was the only respectable path forward.

Those are very different businesses.
